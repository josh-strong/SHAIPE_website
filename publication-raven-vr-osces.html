An examiner-conditioned AI second marker for VR OSCEs

Clinical exams are high-stakes, but a student's fate often rests on one examiner's judgement. RAVEN offers a second perspective — grounded in what actually happened.

Harry Rogers, Sally Shiels, Ashley Tomlinson, James Thomas, Nathan Gauge, James Aylward, Sajan B. Patel, Angela Feixue Wang, Helen Higham and Alison Noble

npj Digital Medicine · 7 October 2026 · Open access

Why a second marker?

In an OSCE, a student walks into a clinical station and is judged — usually by one examiner. That works well for clear passes and clear fails, but plenty of performances land somewhere in between. A second opinion would help, except second markers are expensive and often impractical.

RAVEN (Rater-Aware Verification Network) is built for exactly those ambiguous cases. It watches what the student did in a VR paediatric scenario, reads the examiner's marks and reasoning, and offers an independent second view. Crucially, it doesn't treat all examiners as interchangeable — it accounts for who marked the student and how.

How RAVEN works

The model draws on three sources of evidence: first-person VR video of the encounter, timestamped logs of every action the student took, and the examiner's marks together with any verbal reasoning they recorded. Those inputs are fused and conditioned on the specific examiner, so the AI reviews this particular assessment rather than treating every examiner's standards as identical.

A confidence threshold then decides what happens. When the model is confident its judgement adds something, it contributes to a combined score. When it isn't, the examiner's original mark stands. In practice, this means RAVEN speaks up on the difficult calls and stays quiet on the straightforward ones.

This study was retrospective — no live exam outcomes were changed. The intended role for RAVEN is flagging cases for human review, not overriding examiners.

What the study found

  • Pass/fail agreement rose by 6.2 percentage points. With confidence gating, Gwet's AC1 agreement with the rest of the examiner panel went from 70.0% to 76.2%.
  • Domain-level marking improved too. Mean Gwet's AC2 across all eight clinical domains increased from 78.5% to 81.5%, with statistically significant gains in five of the eight.
  • The biggest difference was at the boundary. For borderline-fail cases — the ones that keep exam boards up at night — agreement with panel consensus jumped from 57.0% to 73.3%.

A note on what these numbers mean. They measure agreement with an examiner panel, not accuracy against some independent ground truth. The panel consensus is the best available reference, but it is still a human judgement.

Results from the paper's retrospective evaluation. AC1 and AC2 are chance-adjusted agreement statistics.

What this means for assessment

The interesting thing isn't just the numbers — it's where the gains appeared. RAVEN helped most when human judgement was already uncertain, exactly the cases where a second opinion has the most value. It also surfaced marking practices that the rubric didn't explicitly describe, giving examiners and exam designers something concrete to discuss.

There's a long way to go before this could be routine. Cross-cohort performance was less consistent than pooled results, prospective trials haven't happened yet, and questions about governance and examiner trust remain open. The paper is deliberately cautious: RAVEN is positioned as an audit and flagging tool, not an autonomous marker.

Behind the paper

Q&A with the author

Harry Rogers

First and corresponding author

Harry Rogers on what motivated RAVEN, what the borderline cases revealed and what comes next.

What problem led you to develop RAVEN?

RAVEN was motivated by a reliability problem in OSCEs: a high-stakes performance is often judged by a single examiner, yet examiners can differ in what they notice, how they interpret criteria, and how they weight the same behaviours. That variation is difficult to audit because conventional OSCEs do not usually provide an objective record of exactly what the candidate did and when.

VR gave us that record, through timestamped actions and video, so we asked whether AI could act as an examiner-conditioned second marker—not replacing the examiner, but checking their judgement against the candidate's behaviour and providing a second source of evidence when appropriate.

What did you learn from the borderline cases?

The clearest benefit of RAVEN was at the pass/fail boundary. For borderline-fail cases, agreement with the leave-one-examiner-out consensus increased from 57.0% to 73.3%, a 16.3 percentage-point improvement.

Of 37 cases where the panel consensus disagreed with the examiner's fail, RAVEN corrected 17. Another 20 potential corrections were left unchanged because the model was not confident enough to intervene.

That taught us two things. First, AI appears most useful where human judgement is already uncertain, rather than on obvious passes or fails. Second, confidence gating matters: the value came from intervening selectively, not from allowing the model to override every disagreement.

What would you want to test before using it in routine exams?

The next step would be a prospective human-in-the-loop study. Rather than automatically changing marks, RAVEN would flag cases and examiners would decide whether to accept, reject or investigate the AI's evidence. We would want to test whether those flags actually improve agreement and decision quality once they begin influencing examiner behaviour.

We would also need external validation across different institutions, specialties, scenarios and OSCE formats, particularly because cross-cohort performance in the study was weaker than pooled performance. I would also want stronger reference panels for difficult cases, examiner training on how to interpret AI flags, explicit governance and appeals processes.

The paper's conclusion is deliberately conservative: until those prospective and external validations are done, RAVEN should be treated as an audit and flagging tool for human adjudication, not as an autonomous marker.
